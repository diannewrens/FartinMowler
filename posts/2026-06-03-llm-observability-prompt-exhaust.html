LLM observability and the smell of prompt exhaust

LLM observability sounds like the adult finally entered the room. The app no longer flings prompts into a model and hopes the smoke comes out shaped like customer value. Now there are traces, spans, token counts, prompt versions, tool calls, latency histograms, cost charts, and dashboards with tasteful purple gradients. Wonderful. We have attached gauges to the pipe.

The smell starts when teams treat the gauges as understanding. A trace can show that the model called search, read three chunks, spent 2.8 seconds thinking, and returned valid JSON. It cannot, by itself, tell you whether the answer helped the user, violated a product rule, contradicted yesterday's answer, or quietly trained a support team to trust decorative nonsense.

If your LLM dashboard knows every token but not whether the customer solved the problem, you are observing exhaust.

Short version

  • LLM observability is useful for tracing prompts, retrieval, tools, latency, cost, and model behavior.
  • It gets smelly when token plumbing becomes a substitute for product outcomes and operational judgment.
  • The fix is to connect traces to user tasks, policy decisions, evals, incidents, and feedback loops.
  • Measure the fart, yes. Then find out who had to sit in the room with it.

The smell

The first dashboard is usually honest. It answers basic questions teams did not have before: which prompt version ran, which model responded, which documents were retrieved, which tools were called, how much it cost, and where latency piled up. This is real progress. Before that, many AI features were production systems with the forensic depth of a wet napkin.

Then the dashboard becomes theatre. A product review shows a tidy trace waterfall. The red span explains the bad answer. The green spans imply everything else is fine. Someone points at a cost chart and says usage is healthy. Someone else says hallucination rate is down because the classifier said so. The room nods. Nobody asks whether users are abandoning the workflow, rewriting the answer, calling support anyway, or developing the haunted habit of asking the same question five different ways until the machine stops being weird.

This is prompt exhaust observability. The system watches what the model emitted and what the orchestration layer did around it, but it does not close the loop on whether the work was correct, safe, useful, or worth doing. It is like putting a very serious sensor on a restaurant kitchen vent and declaring dinner successful because the smoke had acceptable p95 latency.

Why it happens

LLM systems are annoyingly hard to observe because the failure is often semantic. A normal service fails loudly. It times out, throws an exception, drops a queue, or returns the wrong status code. An LLM feature can fail politely. It answers with confidence, uses the right schema, cites a document, and still tells a customer to do the wrong thing with their account. The machine did not crash. It merely produced a small professional lie.

Engineering teams reach for the telemetry they can capture cheaply. Prompts are strings. Tokens are numbers. Retrieval chunks have IDs. Tool calls have arguments. Latency is a histogram. Cost is a chart that makes finance blink. These are easy to instrument, so they become the visible truth. User confusion, policy drift, missing context, bad escalation, and answer usefulness are harder to count, so they become anecdotes. In many companies, anecdotes have to fight dashboards while wearing slippers.

There is also a vendor incentive problem. Observability tools can display traces immediately. They can sell dashboards immediately. They can classify outputs immediately. They cannot magically know your domain, your product promise, your support policy, your legal edge cases, or the difference between a technically plausible answer and the answer your business is willing to stand behind. So the tool shows the part it can see. Fair enough. The problem is when the team mistakes the visible part for the whole animal.

The nastiest trap is correlation without ownership. A trace shows that version 17 of the refund prompt produced more escalations. Is that because the prompt got worse, retrieval changed, the policy page was stale, a tool returned partial data, users asked harder questions, or support changed the routing label? The trace is evidence. It is not a verdict. If no one owns the causal investigation, the dashboard becomes a gorgeous wall of shrug.

The pattern

Start with the user task, not the prompt. Name the job the AI feature is supposed to complete: resolve a billing question, draft a safe reply, classify a bug report, summarize an incident, recommend a migration step. For each job, define what good means in plain operational terms. Correct answer. Safe policy. Useful next step. Required citation. Escalation when evidence is thin. No action when the system is outside its lane.

Then wire the trace to that job. A useful LLM trace should connect prompt version, model, retrieval sources, tool calls, policy checks, output, user action, downstream ticket state, human override, and any later correction. You do not need perfect truth for every interaction. You do need enough receipts to debug patterns instead of vibes. Otherwise your postmortem says "model quality regressed," which is just the software equivalent of blaming the smell on the couch.

Separate plumbing metrics from quality metrics. Plumbing metrics tell you whether the system ran: latency, cost, token volume, tool errors, retrieval misses, schema failures. Quality metrics tell you whether the system helped: task completion, answer acceptance, human edit distance, escalation accuracy, policy violations, repeated-question loops, and sampled expert review. Put them near each other, but do not let one impersonate the other.

Use evals as probes, not perfume. A small eval suite tied to known tasks, current policies, and recent failures is more useful than a giant leaderboard-shaped ceremony. Feed incidents, support corrections, rejected answers, and expert reviews back into the eval set. Version it. Retire stale cases. Track regressions by task type. If your eval cannot explain a real customer failure from last week, it is probably decorating the dashboard.

Finally, give the dashboard an owner with teeth. Someone should be responsible for investigating regressions, changing prompts, updating retrieval, fixing tools, tuning escalation, and saying "we do not know yet." Without that owner, observability becomes a museum. Interesting artifacts. Nice lighting. Nobody repairs the animal making the noise behind the wall.

The Fart Test

  • Can every trace be tied to a named user task or business workflow?
  • Do you track whether the answer helped, or only whether the model produced one?
  • Can you separate latency, cost, and token volume from correctness, safety, and usefulness?
  • Do bad outputs become eval cases, policy fixes, retrieval changes, or tool repairs?
  • Who owns the dashboard when it shows a pattern nobody wants to investigate?

LLM observability is necessary. Please keep the traces, spans, costs, prompts, and weird little classifier labels. Just do not confuse instrumenting the exhaust pipe with understanding the engine, the driver, or the poor customer standing behind the vehicle. If the dashboard smells complete but the workflow still stinks, open the product window.
